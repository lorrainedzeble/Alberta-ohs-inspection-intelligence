# Data cautions

Known traps in this dataset. Each one was found by actually inspecting the live file, not assumed from the vendor docx. Referenced from CLAUDE.md and `data-dictionary.md`; do not silently work around any of these, surface them in skill output when they apply.

## COR_YEAR is completely empty in this file
`COR_YEAR` in the Injury sheet is meant to record whether an employer held a Certificate of Recognition in a given calendar year. Checked directly with pandas on 2026.07.09: `inj["COR_YEAR"].notna().sum()` returns 0 out of 841,761 rows. Every value is blank. This is not a dateless field to work around, there is no COR data anywhere in this file at all. No skill may make any COR claim, including "held in year X." `TRADE_NAME` on the same sheet is equally empty (0 of 841,761) and should also never be referenced.

## Acceptance/Approval status is not reliably synced to its own dates
`DESCRIPTION` in Acceptance and Approval is a status field (Issued, Expired, Revoked, Denied, Suspended, Amended/Varied), and it looks like it should let you infer whether an instrument is still active by comparing `EXPIRY_DATE` to today. It does not work that way:
- 425 of 435 Acceptance rows marked Revoked still show an `EXPIRY_DATE` in the future. Revoked means the Minister cancelled the instrument before or regardless of its original term; the expiry date shown is the original scheduled term, not the actual end of validity.
- 94 of 122 Acceptance rows marked Issued already have an `EXPIRY_DATE` in the past. The status field is not being kept in sync with the passage of time for every row.

Conclusion: only ever report the recorded `DESCRIPTION` status as a historical fact ("this instrument's most recently recorded status is Revoked, granted [date], originally scheduled to expire [date]"). Never infer current validity, and never build any "expiring soon" logic on these dates, for the same reason the COR expiry idea was rejected.

## A rate-direction-only flag misses fatalities, and a tiny base can fake a "trend"
Found during the first real `/quarterly-cycle` run on 2026.07.09. `industry-trend-briefing`'s original `--all` mode flagged an industry only on LTC rate direction ("up" vs "down"). That one signal has two independent failure modes, discovered by actually checking industries a real user expected to see flagged:

**Fatalities can rise while the rate looks fine.** Mobile Equipment Operation's LTC rate improved (0.86 -> 0.66 per 100 person-years, 2020-2024) while its fatality count hit a 5-year high in 2024 (4, 5, 3, 2, **7**). Upstream Oil/Gas, Mobile Equipment Dealers, and Industrial/Commercial Construction all showed real, sometimes spiking fatality counts (7, 9, and 8 total respectively across the 5 years) while their LTC-rate direction alone said "improving" or wasn't checked at all. None of these four appeared in the first real scan's 99 flagged industries. Fatalities move a rate calculation so little (they are rare, low-count events against tens of thousands of person-years) that a rate-only flag is structurally blind to them.

**Direction alone, with no materiality gate, flags noise.** Engineering's LTC rate moved from 0.049 to 0.079 (2020 to 2024), technically "up," but that is a 0.030 percentage-point change on 16 to 36 total LTC claims a year against 30,000+ person-years, noise, not a trend. Quality Control Services - Construction similarly moved 0.199 to 0.266, a 0.067 point change on 7 to 21 claims a year. Both were flagged in the first real scan; neither should have been.

**The fix, both halves independent, never merged:**
- `ltc_di_trend_flag` now requires direction "up" AND an absolute change of at least 0.15 percentage points (originally checked between the earliest and latest stable year; see the follow-up section below for why that itself was replaced with pooled 2-year windows). 0.15 sits comfortably above both known noise cases (0.030 and 0.067) while still catching genuine movers. This threshold is a judgment call, stated here so it can be revisited, not a law of nature.
- `fatality_flag` is a separate, always-computed field: true if the industry (or employer, in single-industry mode) recorded at least one fatality in 2023 or 2024, the two most recent years. This is a historical fact check, not a trend comparison and not a forecast, consistent with CLAUDE.md refusal 3 (never predict a future fatality). It fires independently of whatever the LTC/DI rate is doing.

An industry can carry either flag, both, or neither. Never collapse them into one "flagged" boolean; always show `flagged_because` so the analyst knows which signal fired.

## A first-year-vs-last-year comparison hides interior volatility and can anchor to an anomalous baseline
Found continuing the same 2026.07.09 use episode, after the materiality fix above. The 40 person-year floor on its own was not the problem in either case below, both industries clear it by a wide margin. The problem was comparing only two single years and ignoring everything between them.

**Iron/Steel Foundries**: person-years 194.5-298 across 2020-2024, comfortably stable, but LTC counts are tiny (4 to 17 claims a year) and the rate is genuinely volatile: 2.056 -> 5.415 -> 4.698 -> 6.13 -> 2.659. Comparing only 2020 (2.056) to 2024 (2.659) calls this a mild "up," when the real story is a mid-series spike that mostly came back down, not a trend in either direction.

**Air Service - Scheduled Commercial**: person-years 1,417-2,423, well above the floor, LTC rate 4.389 -> 7.196 -> 7.177 -> 5.901 -> 5.722. This industry has been declining for three straight years since its 2021 peak, but comparing 2020 to 2024 called it "up" (+1.333), because 2020 was an unusually low starting point, plausibly a pandemic-affected year, the same kind of 2020 anomaly that distorted Engineering's numbers in the materiality check above. Anchoring a trend to 2020 specifically is risky across this whole dataset for that reason.

**The fix:** `ltc_di_trend_direction` and `ltc_di_trend_flag` now come from `_pooled_ltc_trend()`, which sums raw counts and person years across the earliest 2 stable years and the latest 2 stable years separately (sum first, one rate each side, the same rule used everywhere else in this dataset), then compares those two pooled rates instead of two single years. Re-run on the same two industries: Iron/Steel Foundries moves from a misleading "up" to a real, moderate one (pooled early rate 3.974, pooled late rate 4.572, delta +0.598); Air Service moves from a sharp false "up" (+1.333) to essentially flat (pooled early rate 5.662, pooled late rate 5.816, delta +0.154), correctly showing a plateau instead of a rise. Requires at least 4 stable years so the two windows never overlap; fewer than that reports `insufficient_stable_years_for_pooled_trend` rather than approximating, the same convention `enforcement-effectiveness` uses for its own before/after windows.

## Employer count is not the same as workforce size, and a few industries have stale data
Found continuing the same 2026.07.09 `/quarterly-cycle` use episode, checking a small-employer-count slice of flagged industries to see if the large/small pattern above held.

**`latest_employer_count` can badly understate scale.** "GOVERNMENT OF ALBERTA" shows `latest_employer_count: 2`, which looks tiny next to industries with thousands of employers, but its actual person-years are about 29,700-29,800, comparable to some of the largest industries in the dataset. Two WCB accounts can cover an enormous consolidated workforce. Never use `latest_employer_count` alone as a size signal without also checking `person_years_sum`; it is a count of registered accounts, not a headcount.

**3 of 302 industries only have data through 2021, not 2024.** Government of Alberta, Health Care Services - Alberta Health Services, and Health Care Services - Covenant Health all show `years_covered: [2020, 2021]` only, most likely reclassified under different `WCB_INDUSTRY_NAME` values afterward (not checked further; the effect on this workspace is the same either way). Their `ltc_di_trend_flag`/`fatality_flag` values are computed correctly from the years they actually have, but presenting them next to industries current through 2024 without saying so is misleading, a 2020-vs-2021 comparison is not the same recency as a 2020-vs-2024 one. `latest_year_is_current` is set on every industry's record specifically so this can't be silently missed; check it before presenting any flagged industry, and say so plainly when it is false.

## Order STATUS does not mean what it superficially looks like it means
`STATUS` on the Order sheet is not an open/resolved binary. Real distribution across 71,755 rows: Compliance (70,662, 98.5%), Suspension (547), Rescission (341), Non-Compliance (140), Order Revoked (23), Open (17), plus a couple of single digit categories. Reading this naively, "Compliance" looks like it could mean "waiting on compliance," but at 98.5% of all rows it almost certainly means the order was complied with, a resolved positive outcome, not a pending one. `Open` (17 rows) is genuinely rare, an order actually sitting unresolved right now is not a normal state in this data. `Non-Compliance` (140 rows) is the real "still a problem" signal.

Any skill logic that needs "an order that is not resolved" must define it explicitly as `STATUS in ("Non-Compliance", "Open")`, never as a vague "not yet complied with" framing that could accidentally include the 98.5% "Compliance" majority. Same species of trap as the Acceptance/Approval status desync above: a status field whose plain English label does not mean what it looks like it means until you check the distribution.

## Penalty's INDUSTRY_SECTOR, verified against Injury's WCB_INDUSTRY_NAME
Checked directly on 2026.07.09: 45 of 48 unique Penalty `INDUSTRY_SECTOR` values match an Injury `WCB_INDUSTRY_NAME` value exactly; the 3 that don't ("FEED LOTS", "FOR ADMINISTRATIVE PURPOSES", "INSPECTION SERVICES- VISUAL, NEC") look like legitimate Penalty specific categories, not disagreement. Of the 80 Penalty rows whose `EMP_KEY` also has an Injury row, zero showed a mismatched industry. So the two fields agree wherever both exist. The real gap: 14 of 94 Penalty employers (15%) have no Injury row at all, so there is nothing to join back to for them.

Rule: attribute industry via the `EMP_KEY` join back to Injury's `WCB_INDUSTRY_NAME` as the primary source, since Injury is the complete, authoritative table. Fall back to Penalty's own `INDUSTRY_SECTOR` only for the employers with no Injury row, since it has been verified reliable wherever it was checkable. Don't silently drop those 14 employers from an industry level rollup just because they lack an Injury row.

## Investigation has no date field at all
Not a missing value on some rows, the sheet has no date or year column whatsoever, only `EMP_KEY`, `EMPLOYER_NAME`, `DESCRIPTION`, `URL`. It can only ever be used as a binary "ever investigated" flag. It cannot feed a trend, a "last N years" window, or a "first enforcement action" calculation. Do not approximate a date from the description text.

## Rates are unstable under 40 person years
`LOST_TIME_CLAIM_RATE` and `DISABLING_INJURIES_RATE` are both per 100 person years. Per the vendor docx, a rate computed from fewer than 40 person years worked is too volatile to compare meaningfully and should be flagged, not reported as a stable fact.

## EMP_KEY, not employer name, is the join key
Employer name is present on every sheet for display, but it is not guaranteed unique or stable. Two different `EMP_KEY` values can show the same or a very similar employer name (name changes, re-registration, punctuation differences). Always join and de duplicate on `EMP_KEY`; if a name maps to more than one key, or a key maps to more than one name, flag it in the output rather than silently picking one.

## First enforcement action needs one definition across four date formats
"First enforcement action" for an employer requires comparing dates across Order, Ticket, Penalty, and Conviction, and those four sheets do not store dates the same way. The action date to use per sheet is `Order.ISSUE_DATE`, `Ticket.OFFENCE_DATE`, `Penalty.SERVED_DATE`, and `Conviction.DATE_OF_CONVICTION`, specifically not `Conviction.INCIDENT_DATE`, since the incident date is the underlying event and the conviction date is when OHS actually acted, sometimes years later (see the Suncor sample row in `data-profile.md`, incident Jan 2021, conviction Apr 2023). Order and Ticket dates are Excel serials, Penalty and Conviction dates are pre-formatted strings; normalize all four to real dates first, union them into one `(EMP_KEY, action_date, source_sheet)` table. `enforcement-effectiveness` takes the minimum per `EMP_KEY` (first action, for the before/after split). `enforcement-gap-watchlist` takes the maximum per `EMP_KEY` (most recent action, to check nothing happened in the last 3+ years). Build the unified date table once as shared logic; both skills read from it and must not normalize these four date formats two different ways.

## Before/after windows are limited by only 5 years of data
The dataset only covers 2020 to 2024, so an employer whose first enforcement action was in 2023 has just one possible "after" year (2024) and one whose first action was in 2020 has zero "before" years. Rule: compare up to 2 years before and up to 2 years after the action year (excluding the action year itself), using whatever years actually exist in range. For a single employer, if either side has zero available years, state "insufficient data, before/after not computable" for that employer rather than compute a number on an empty or single point window. For an industry level rollup, sum raw counts and person years across whatever years are available in each window across all employers, then compute one pooled rate, the same sum-first rule used for person year stability elsewhere in this file; do not average uneven per employer windows together.

## Date fields are not consistently typed
`ISSUE_DATE` (Order, Acceptance, Approval), `EXPIRY_DATE` (Acceptance, Approval), and `OFFENCE_DATE` (Ticket) come through pandas as raw Excel serial numbers (for example 44282.77), not parsed dates, because the source file never applied a date number format to those cells. Convert explicitly with `pd.Timestamp("1899-12-30") + pd.to_timedelta(serial, unit="D")` before using them. By contrast, Penalty's `SERVED_DATE` and Conviction's `INCIDENT_DATE`/`DATE_OF_CONVICTION` already come through as formatted text like "Nov 09, 2020". Don't assume one date handling approach works for every sheet.

## Legacy text encoding artifacts in narrative fields
Conviction's `DESCRIPTION` and `CONTRAVENTION` fields contain stray characters where apostrophes should be (a Windows-1252 to UTF-8 mismatch upstream). Neither field is surfaced by any skill in this workspace, so this does not currently need a fix, but if that ever changes, clean the encoding first rather than displaying it as is.

## A higher raw rate is not the same as beating random by more than chance (dated 2026.07.10)
`inspection-target-ranking`'s back test originally gated only on `top_rate > pop_rate`, a strict comparison of two observed rates with no accounting for sampling variance. At `top_n = 10`, that is a weak bar: three real industries from a real Step 3 run passed it while having an 18 to 27% chance of doing so on a purely random top-10 draw at the population's own rate, checked directly with the binomial tail probability P(X>=observed hits | Binomial(top_n, pop_rate)):
- Industrial/Commercial Construction: pop rate 2.01%, 1/10 hits, 18.4% chance at random.
- General Automotive Repairs/Auto Wreckers: pop rate 18.15%, 3/10 hits, 26.7% chance at random.
- Construction Framing Contractor: pop rate 14.93%, 3/10 hits, 17.8% chance at random.

All three would have passed the old gate as "beats random sampling," language that implies more than a single lucky-looking draw. The other 11 industries in that same run all had a chance probability under 2%, genuinely unlikely to be noise.

**Fix applied:** the back test now also requires `chance_probability_of_this_hit_rate_at_random < 0.05` (`BACKTEST_SIGNIFICANCE_THRESHOLD` in `inspection-target-ranking/script.py`) before `gated_exception_triggered` is False. Both conditions must hold: the top-N rate must exceed the population rate, and that gap must be unlikely at the 0.05 level under the population's own rate treated as the chance baseline. 0.05 is a disclosed, conventional threshold, not derived from this dataset; if the analyst wants a stricter or looser bar, change `BACKTEST_SIGNIFICANCE_THRESHOLD` and say so, don't improvise a different number in a response.

This does not change the Field Production Operators rejection (it failed the raw-rate comparison outright, 0.0% top rate against a 0.02% population rate, so it was never in question), but it means the three industries above must be re-backtested and will very likely also gate as rejected, or as "sorted by raw indicators only," under the corrected rule.

## 342 EMP_KEYs are recorded under two different employer names, causing real double-counting (dated 2026.07.10)
Checked directly after a Step 4 employer briefing spot check surfaced a duplicated-looking trend for one employer: 342 of 236,365 distinct `EMP_KEY` values (0.145%) have more than one distinct `EMPLOYER_NAME` in the raw Injury sheet, and in every one of these 342 cases (not a handful, all of them) the two names appear within the *same year*, not as a rename over time. The pattern is consistent: a numbered corporation paired with an operating name, for example `EMP_KEY 80ADDCBE-6258-F719-CC4E-81F42334B96D` appears as both "NORTHWEST SPRING & MACHINE INC." and "TRUCK ZONE INC.," and `01436142-BFD0-F247-77F5-269A483F8E51` appears as both "2290682 ALBERTA LTD." and "OG DRYWALL LTD." This is almost certainly a legal-name/trade-name pair sharing one WCB account; `TRADE_NAME` itself is completely empty in this file (see the COR/TRADE_NAME caution above), so this dual-labeling under `EMPLOYER_NAME` is the closest thing to trade-name information actually present.

Checked whether it is safe to treat these as true duplicate rows: for every one of the 1,288 same-EMP_KEY-same-year duplicate groups, `PERSON_YEARS_COUNT`, `LOST_TIME_CLAIM_COUNT`, and every other numeric injury field are identical between the name-variant rows. 13 of the 1,288 groups (across a subset of the 342 EMP_KEYs) additionally carry two different `CITY_NAME` values alongside the two names (for example one EMP_KEY shows both Sherwood Park and Calgary), meaning for those 13 employers city, not just name, is ambiguous; the injury metrics themselves are still identical across all rows in every one of these 13 groups too.

Left unaddressed, this caused two distinct problems:
1. **Silent double-counting.** Any code that sums raw Injury rows without first deduplicating by `EMP_KEY`+`YEAR_NO` counted these 342 employers' contribution twice: `industry-trend-briefing`'s industry-level sums (which sum across every row in an industry-year, not grouped by employer first) and `inspection-target-ranking`'s `_build_features()` (`groupby("EMP_KEY").agg(sum)`, which would sum two identical rows per EMP_KEY into one, doubling `recent_ltc_count`, `recent_di_count`, and `recent_person_years` for these 342 employers specifically). This was invisible to the pinned Roofing gold example's own verification, since the independent `pd.read_excel` recomputation summed the same raw, undeduplicated rows the same way; both sides of that check inherited the same duplication.
2. **Silent name-picking.** `employer-briefing` and `inspection-target-ranking` each resolved one employer name per `EMP_KEY` (`.iloc[-1]` / `.last()`) with no indication that a second, entirely different-looking business name exists for that same account. CLAUDE.md's Governance section already states "Escalate to the analyst on: EMP_KEY/name mismatches" as a standing rule, but neither skill actually implemented a check for it before this fix, a real gap between a documented rule and the code.

**Fix applied:** `ohs_data.py`'s `load_injury()` now deduplicates on `(EMP_KEY, YEAR_NO)`, keeping one row, since every numeric field is confirmed identical across the duplicate rows. A new `ambiguous_employer_names()` function returns the full list of names on file for any `EMP_KEY` with more than one, built from the raw undeduplicated sheet. `employer-briefing` now surfaces `employer_name_ambiguous` and `employer_name_all_known_names` in its output; `inspection-target-ranking` surfaces `employer_name_ambiguous` per shortlisted employer in both `rank()` and `backtest()`'s underlying features. Neither skill picks one name silently anymore without flagging that a second one exists. The 13-employer city ambiguity is documented here but not separately flagged in code, given its narrow scope (13 of 236,365 employers, 0.0055%); an analyst who checks `employer-briefing`'s full enforcement history for a flagged EMP_KEY will still see every recorded city in the underlying Order/Ticket/Penalty rows regardless.

## Five real bugs surfaced by an adversarial live-use critic (dated 2026.07.11)
A critic briefed to role-play the analyst with a real live question, actually operate the workspace rather than review its code, and try to break it, found five real problems on an ordinary first use, not under contrived attack. Each is fixed here; none was hypothetical.

**CITY_NAME is a mailing address, not a confirmed Alberta worksite.** Ranking a real flagged industry live grouped its top-10 shortlist under Mississauga, Richmond, Lachine, Winnipeg, and Brampton, only one Alberta city among them; the file has no province or worksite field at all (checked directly: 2,516 distinct city values, only a handful matching even a generous curated list of known Alberta municipalities), so a national or multi-location employer's registered mailing city can be an out-of-province head office. There is no reliable way to auto-classify 2,516 free-text city strings against Alberta municipalities without a real gazetteer, and a wrong guess (falsely flagging a legitimate small Alberta town) is worse than not guessing. **Fix:** stated as an explicit, always-shown caveat in `inspection-target-ranking/SKILL.md` and CLAUDE.md rather than silently auto-classified; the analyst must confirm the employer's actual Alberta worksite location before routing an inspection off this shortlist's city grouping.

**Raw unconverted Excel date serials reached the analyst in `employer-briefing`'s enforcement history.** `_sheet_history()` selected the raw `ISSUE_DATE`/`OFFENCE_DATE` columns instead of the `ACTION_DATE` field `ohs_data.py` already computes correctly for every sheet; a live dossier for a real flagged employer (Flynn Canada Ltd.) showed `"ISSUE_DATE": 43902.5715625` instead of a date. Independently converted (`pd.Timestamp("1899-12-30") + pd.to_timedelta(43902.5715625, unit="D")` = `2020-03-12`) to confirm this was the only problem, not a wrong date. **Fix:** `employer-briefing/script.py`'s `_sheet_history()` now always displays the normalized `ACTION_DATE` regardless of which sheet's raw column was originally serial (Order, Ticket) versus already-formatted text (Penalty, Conviction); Conviction's incident date is kept alongside the normalized conviction date rather than dropped, since the two are meaningfully different (see the "First enforcement action" section above).

**`enforcement-gap-watchlist` dropped the source sheet before it reached the analyst.** `most_recent_enforcement_action_by_employer()` returns `EMP_KEY`/`ACTION_DATE`/`SOURCE_SHEET`, but `watchlist()` indexed only `["ACTION_DATE"]`, discarding which of Order/Ticket/Penalty/Conviction the date came from, a direct violation of this workspace's own citation convention (every fact needs a source sheet, not just a date). **Fix:** `watchlist()` now also returns `most_recent_enforcement_action_source_sheet` per flagged employer.

**Look-ahead leakage inside the ranking's own validation gate.** `_build_features(industry, city, as_of_year)` correctly restricted `recent_ltc_count`, `recent_di_count`, and `fatality_ever` to years `<= as_of_year`, but `unresolved_order_ever` (weight 3.0) and `acceptance_approval_concern` (weight 1.0) came from `ohs.unresolved_orders()`/`ohs.acceptance_approval_concern_employers()`, neither of which accepted or applied `as_of_year`, so both always read the full 2020-2024 Order/Acceptance/Approval sheets regardless of the scoring cutoff. In the back test (scored as of 2022, checked against 2023-2024 outcomes), an employer with a 2023 or 2024 order got scoring credit from that same future order, and that same order is what counts it as a "hit" in the outcome check, a real risk of circularity in the exact mechanism this workspace tells the analyst to trust before presenting a ranking as validated. **Fix:** both `unresolved_orders()` and `acceptance_approval_concern_employers()` now accept an `as_of_year` parameter and filter to actions/instruments issued on or before that year; `inspection-target-ranking/script.py`'s `_build_features()` now passes `as_of_year` through to both. All 15 previously-decided back tests were rerun after this fix; see `evaluation/use-log.md` for the before/after verdicts.

**A misspelled industry name's error message pointed to a resource that can't answer the question.** `industry-trend-briefing/script.py` told the operator to check `knowledge/data-dictionary.md` for the correct spelling, but that file documents the field's type and meaning, not its 302 real values; following the error message's own advice leads nowhere. **Fix:** the error now suggests live close matches (`difflib.get_close_matches`) computed directly from the actual `WCB_INDUSTRY_NAME` values in the loaded data, so the suggestion can never go stale relative to the source file.

**Residual, stated rather than fixed:** the same critic confirmed the `/quarterly-cycle` workflow's Step 2 analyst-confirmation pause is enforced by instruction only, not by any code-level mechanism; it was walked through with zero human input on direct test. See CLAUDE.md's Governance section and `.claude/commands/quarterly-cycle.md`'s Step 2 for the honest statement of this limitation.

## Ties on TOTAL_SCORE were not broken deterministically (dated 2026.07.12)
Rechecking whether the look-ahead-leakage fix above (2026.07.11, `unresolved_order_ever`/`acceptance_approval_concern` now respecting `as_of_year`) changed any of the 10 validated industries' actual `rank()` shortlists, not just the backtest's pass/fail verdict, surfaced a real, previously-unknown bug: rerunning `rank(as_of_year=2024, top_n=10)` after the fix showed a different 3rd-place employer in 2 of the 10 industries. Checked directly, not assumed: in both cases the swapped employers had byte-identical `TOTAL_SCORE` and byte-identical scoring components (`recent_ltc_count`, `recent_di_count`, `fatality_ever`, `unresolved_order_ever`, `acceptance_approval_concern`), only `recent_person_years` differed, which is not part of the score formula. Upstream Oil/Gas: Tidewater Midstream and Paramount Resources both score 13.0. Mechanical Contracting: Jetco Mechanical and Petrin Mechanical both score 30.0.

`_score(features).sort_values("TOTAL_SCORE", ascending=False)` had no tie-breaker, so which of two exactly-tied employers lands in a "top N" cutoff depended on the incidental row order of `features` at sort time, not on anything about their actual risk profile. That row order can shift from any unrelated code change (for example the EMP_KEY-dedup fix from 2026.07.10 altered `load_injury()`'s row order), meaning two runs of identical business logic could show the analyst a different specific employer with no real difference in risk, and the choice was not reproducible.

**Fix applied:** `rank()` and `backtest()` both now sort on `["TOTAL_SCORE", "EMP_KEY"]` (`ascending=[False, True]`, `kind="stable"`). `EMP_KEY` is a deterministic, arbitrary tie-break, not a judgment about which tied employer is more worth visiting; it exists only to make the same inputs always produce the same output. Re-ran all 15 previously-decided back tests after this fix: unchanged (10 validated, 5 rejected, same population sizes and chance probabilities), since a tie-break only reorders exactly-tied rows and does not change any employer's actual score or their presence in the outcome-rate calculation.

This is the ninth real, independently-verified fix from this workspace's use, found only because a fix's real-world impact was checked by diffing actual shortlists rather than trusting that an unrelated component's fix couldn't have side effects.

## A tied boundary was treated as automatically making the verdict ambiguous, when it usually doesn't (dated 2026.07.13)
The 2026.07.12 fix above (deterministic tie-break) and the systemic finding that followed it (9 of 15 industries have a boundary tie) led to a `deferred_boundary_tie` verdict for all 9, on the reasoning that a tied cutoff makes "which employers land in the top N" arbitrary. That reasoning is correct as far as it goes, but the code implementing it stopped one step short: it never asked whether the tie actually changes the pass/fail conclusion, only whether a tie exists at all. Checked directly: Field Production Operators (0 enforcement actions among its top-10 employers, population enforcement rate 0.02%) was deferred alongside Construction Framing Contractor (a genuine 45.3%-vs-1.0% flip), even though no possible resolution of Field Production Operators' 39-way tie could make it pass.

**Fix applied:** `backtest()` now computes the worst-case tie-break (tied slots filled with as few 2023-2024 enforcement hits as possible) and the best-case tie-break (filled with as many hits as possible) explicitly, and only returns `deferred_boundary_tie` (with a new `tie_sensitive: true` field) when the verdict genuinely differs between the two. Re-ran all 15: only 1 of the 9 tied industries (Construction Framing Contractor) is actually tie-sensitive; the other 8 have a real tie that turns out to be immaterial to the conclusion. Corrected cycle result: 10 validated, 4 rejected, 1 deferred, not the previously reported 6 validated, 9 deferred, 0 rejected. Full per-industry worst-case/best-case numbers in `evaluation/use-log.md`, dated 2026-07-13.

**Lesson for any future gated-exception rule of this shape:** a condition that makes an outcome *possibly* wrong ("the input is ambiguous") is not the same as a condition that makes it *actually* wrong ("the ambiguity changes the answer"). Encode the second, testable claim directly (compute both ends of the range and compare), rather than deferring to the first, weaker claim as a shortcut. The first version of this fix mistook "a tie exists" for "the tie matters," and reused the tenth fix's own correct governance-rule prose as if the code already implemented what the prose argued for.

## Same request also asked whether the pinned Roofing gold example had drifted; checked directly, it had not (dated 2026.07.13)
